The economic calendar lists the scheduled events that can move markets: data releases, central bank decisions and speeches. Reading it well is less about predicting numbers and more about knowing when volatility is likely and planning your trades around it.

## The columns explained

- **Time:** when the figure is released. Our [economic calendar](/tools/economic-calendar) shows it in your local time zone.
- **Currency or country:** the economy the release belongs to, and therefore the currencies most likely to react.
- **Impact:** an estimate of how much the event usually moves markets, often shown as low, medium or high.
- **Actual:** the published figure, filled in at release time.
- **Forecast:** the consensus expectation of economists.
- **Previous:** the last reading, sometimes revised.

## Surprises move markets

Markets react to the gap between **actual** and **forecast**, not to whether a number is "good" or "bad" in isolation. If inflation was expected to rise to 3.2% and comes in at 3.4%, the surprise is what matters. Revisions to the previous figure can change the picture too.

## The releases traders watch most

- **Central bank decisions** and the statements and press conferences that follow. See our guide to [how central banks move currencies](/blog/how-central-banks-move-currencies).
- **Inflation (consumer price index).** Shapes expectations for interest rates.
- **Employment reports**, including the monthly US jobs report. Watched for jobs created, the unemployment rate and wage growth.
- **Growth (GDP) and business surveys** such as purchasing managers' indices.
- **Retail sales**, a read on consumer demand.
- **Energy inventories**, which matter for oil traders.

## Which markets react

- Currencies of the releasing country react first and most directly.
- **Gold** often reacts to US inflation and jobs data through the dollar and yields. Read [what drives gold](/blog/trading-gold-what-drives-the-price).
- **Stock indices** react to data that changes the outlook for rates and growth.

## Planning around releases

1. **Check the week ahead** every Monday and mark the high-impact events for the markets you trade.
2. **Decide before the release** whether you will stay out, reduce size or hold existing positions with a plan.
3. **Expect wider spreads and slippage** in the seconds around a release. Stops can fill at worse prices than set.
4. **Do not chase the first spike.** The initial move often partly reverses as the details are digested.
5. **Size from your stop.** If you do trade the news, use the [lot size calculator](/tools/lot-size-calculator) with a realistic stop distance for the volatility.

## A weekly routine

- Monday: list the key events and the instruments they affect.
- Day before each event: check the forecast and the previous figure.
- After the event: note how the market reacted compared with the surprise. Over time this builds a feel for what matters most.

The calendar will not tell you which way the market will go. It tells you when to be most careful, which is often just as valuable.
