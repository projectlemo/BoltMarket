If you trade currencies, central banks are the most important actors you will never meet. Their decisions on interest rates, and what they say about future decisions, set the background for every major pair.

## Why interest rates move currencies

Money tends to flow towards higher returns. When a central bank raises its policy rate, deposits and bonds in that currency usually pay more, which can attract demand for the currency. When it cuts rates, the opposite pressure applies.

What matters most is the **difference** between rates in two economies and how that difference is expected to change. A pair like USD/JPY often reacts strongly to shifts in the gap between US and Japanese rate expectations.

## Expectations matter more than decisions

Markets trade the future. By the time a widely expected rate hike is announced, it is usually already reflected in the price. That is why a currency can **fall** on the day its central bank raises rates: the hike was priced in, and the message about what comes next was less aggressive than traders hoped.

Traders therefore focus on three things on decision day:

1. **The decision** compared with what was expected.
2. **The statement** and any change in wording about future policy.
3. **The press conference**, where answers to questions can shift expectations further.

## Hawkish and dovish

- **Hawkish** signals point to higher rates or a longer period of tight policy. They tend to support the currency.
- **Dovish** signals point to cuts or a longer period of easy policy. They tend to weigh on it.

A single phrase can move a market if it changes how traders read the path ahead.

## Beyond rates: balance sheets and intervention

Central banks also buy or sell large amounts of bonds, which affects longer-term yields. Some occasionally intervene directly in currency markets to slow sharp moves. Interventions are rare and hard to predict, but they can produce very large intraday swings.

## The data that feeds decisions

Central banks react to inflation, employment and growth. That is why releases such as consumer prices and payrolls move currencies even between meetings: they change expectations about the next decision. Our guide to [reading the economic calendar](/blog/reading-the-economic-calendar) explains how to follow them.

## Trading around decisions

- **Know the date and time.** Decisions are scheduled; they appear on the [economic calendar](/tools/economic-calendar).
- **Expect wider spreads** and fast price changes around the announcement.
- **Reduce size or stay flat** if your stop could be skipped by a sudden move.
- **Wait for the dust to settle.** Many traders let the first reaction play out and trade the direction that holds after the press conference.

Central bank policy rarely makes for a quick trade, but understanding it explains most of the big trends in currency markets.
