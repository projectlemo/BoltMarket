Every time a traveller changes money at an airport, a company pays an overseas supplier or a fund buys foreign bonds, one currency is exchanged for another. The foreign exchange market, or forex, is where those prices are set. As a trader, you are not buying banknotes; you are taking a position on whether one currency will strengthen or weaken against another.

## Currencies always trade in pairs

A forex price compares two currencies. In **EUR/USD**, the euro is the *base* currency and the US dollar is the *quote* currency. A price of 1.1600 means one euro costs 1.16 dollars.

- If you **buy** EUR/USD, you expect the euro to rise against the dollar.
- If you **sell** EUR/USD, you expect the euro to fall against the dollar.

Because every trade is relative, there is no such thing as "the dollar going up" in isolation. The dollar can rise against the yen and fall against the pound on the same day.

## Bid, ask and the spread

Your platform always shows two prices. The **bid** is the price at which you can sell; the **ask** is the price at which you can buy. The gap between them is the **spread**, and it is the main cost of most trades. If EUR/USD is quoted at 1.16000 / 1.16015, the spread is 1.5 pips.

That is why a new position starts slightly negative: you buy at the higher price and could only sell immediately at the lower one.

## Why the market never sleeps

Forex has no single exchange. Banks, brokers and institutions trade with each other across time zones, so the market follows the sun from Sydney and Tokyo to London and New York. It opens on Sunday evening (European time) and closes on Friday evening, trading around the clock in between.

Liquidity is not constant, though. The busiest period is usually when the London and New York sessions overlap. Quiet hours and holidays can bring wider spreads and sudden jumps.

## Majors, crosses and exotics

- **Majors** pair the US dollar with another large economy's currency, such as EUR/USD, GBP/USD and USD/JPY. They are the most traded and usually have the tightest spreads.
- **Crosses** pair two major currencies without the dollar, such as EUR/GBP or GBP/JPY.
- **Exotics** pair a major with an emerging-market currency, such as USD/MXN. Spreads are wider and moves can be sharper.

## How profit and loss are calculated

Your result depends on three things: how far the price moves, the size of your position and the value of each pip. On one standard lot of EUR/USD, each pip is worth about $10. A 30-pip move in your favour is roughly $300; the same move against you is a $300 loss. Our [pip calculator](/tools/pip-calculator) does this maths for any pair and account currency.

## Start with a plan

Forex is accessible, but leverage makes it easy to take on far more risk than you intended. Before your first live trade:

1. Practise on a [demo account](/trading/demo-account) until placing and closing orders feels routine.
2. Decide how much of your account you are willing to risk per trade.
3. Always attach a stop-loss, and size the position so that stop equals your planned risk.
